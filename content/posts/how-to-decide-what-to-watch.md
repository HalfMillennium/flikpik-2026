You sat down forty minutes ago. The snacks are open. Nobody has pressed play.

This is the most common way a movie night dies: not from a bad pick, but from no pick at all. Everyone has veto power and nobody has a plan, so you scroll four services in parallel, read three plot summaries out loud, and eventually someone says "I don't care, just put anything on" in a tone that means the opposite.

Here are five ways to end it. The first four work solo or as a couple. The fifth is the only one that reliably works for a group — which is the hard case, and the reason it kept happening to you.

## 1. The two-minute timer

Set a timer for two minutes. Whoever is holding the remote picks in that window. No committee. When it goes off, you press play on whatever's selected.

This works because the problem was never a shortage of good options — it was that open-ended browsing has no stopping rule. A timer is a stopping rule. It's blunt, it occasionally lands you on something mediocre, and it beats forty minutes of nothing every single time.

## 2. Dictator of the week

One person chooses everything tonight. Next time, someone else does. You rotate.

The trick is that it removes negotiation entirely, and because everyone knows their turn is coming, nobody fights the current pick too hard. Over a month it's perfectly fair. On any given night it's a benevolent dictatorship, which is the fastest form of government for choosing a movie.

## 3. Cut the list in half, twice

Pull up six options. Each person kills one they'd hate. You're down to a shortlist fast, and whatever survives has no strong objectors — which matters more than having a strong champion. The movie nobody hates beats the movie one person loves and two people tolerate.

## 4. Shrink the pool before you browse

Most decision fatigue comes from browsing everything ever made. Don't. Decide the constraints *first* — under two hours, something funny, nothing anyone's seen — then browse only inside them. You've turned an infinite problem into a small one, and small problems get solved.

## 5. Everyone votes at once (the group fix)

The methods above all have the same weakness with a group: they hand the decision to one person and hope the rest go along. Someone always quietly resents it.

The alternative is to let everyone vote *simultaneously* on the same shortlist, and play the first movie that gets a majority. No turns. No dictator. No reading summaries aloud. Each person swipes yes or no on the same options at the same time, and the moment enough people agree, you're done.

This is exactly what [flikpik](/) does. You pool everyone's watch lists, the group swipes together in real time, and the first movie to a majority wins. It turns the forty-minute argument into about ninety seconds — and because everyone actually voted, nobody spends the first act feeling railroaded.

## The real lesson

Every method here works by adding one thing the open Netflix menu refuses to give you: a rule that forces a decision. A timer, a rotation, an elimination, a vote. Pick whichever fits your couch. Just pick one before you sit down — because the browsing itself is the thing eating your night.
