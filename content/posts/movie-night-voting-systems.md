Group movie choice is a voting problem, and humanity has invented a lot of voting systems. Here they are from worst to best, judged on two things: how fast they end the argument, and how few people quietly resent the result.

## 6. The coin flip

Two options, heads or tails. Instant, and completely fair in the mathematical sense — which is exactly why it feels bad. A coin ignores that three people mildly wanted one film and one person desperately wanted the other. Fast, but it throws away all the information about how strongly anyone actually cares. Use only as a tiebreaker of last resort.

## 5. Loudest person wins

Not a system so much as the absence of one. Whoever argues hardest, or sulks most effectively, gets their pick. It resolves quickly and it corrodes movie night, because the same person wins every week and everyone else stops suggesting anything. Named here only so you can recognize it and stop doing it.

## 4. Dictator of the week

One person owns the whole decision tonight; the role rotates each session. Fast, and fair *over time* even though any single night is autocratic. Its weakness is memory — it only feels fair if the rotation is real and nobody "forgets" whose turn it was. Great for a fixed group that meets regularly. Useless for a one-off.

## 3. Veto rounds

Everyone gets one veto. Start with a shortlist, each person strikes one title they refuse to watch, and you play whatever survives. This is quietly excellent, because it optimizes for the right thing: not the movie someone loves most, but the movie *no one hates.* For a mixed group, "zero strong objectors" beats "one strong champion" almost every time. Its only limit is that it needs a pre-built shortlist to veto from.

## 2. Ranked choice

Everyone ranks the shortlist; you eliminate the lowest and redistribute until something has a majority. It's the most *information-rich* method here — it captures not just what people want but the shape of their preferences. The catch is overhead: ranking five films by hand, tallying rounds, explaining instant-runoff to the one friend who always asks how it works. Correct, and slightly too much work for a Tuesday.

## 1. Swipe consensus

Everyone swipes yes/no on the same shortlist at the same time, and the first movie to reach a majority wins. It's the fastest method that still uses everyone's real preferences — no turns to wait through, no ranking homework, no dictator to resent. Simultaneous voting kills the politeness loop (the "no, *you* pick" spiral) because nobody has to volunteer a decision; you just register your own yes-or-no and the math resolves it.

## The pattern

Notice the ranking tracks one variable: how much a method respects *everyone's* preference while still forcing a result. Coin flips force a result but ignore preference. Loudest-wins respects one preference and ignores the rest. The good methods — vetoes, ranked choice, swipe — all find the option the group can live with, and the best ones do it without homework.
